The Math of Staying

Why working is a losing bet when the system is designed to filter you out

I spent the last few months running the numbers on something most people never have to think about: whether it's worth it to work. Not whether work has value in a philosophical sense. Whether the arithmetic works. Whether, after taxes, insurance, lost benefits, lost housing, and the administrative nightmare of holding it all together, you actually come out ahead.

The answer, for me, ended up being no. Not because I don't want to work. Because the system punishes you for trying.

The scenario

The only realistic shot I had at a decent income was working full time for Matt at $50 an hour. Normal trainer wages. That comes out to roughly $117,000 a year. The math after that looks like this:

Take-home pay$82,700

Then you take out health insurance. Roughly $17,000 a year for a marketplace plan in California. That drops you to about $65,700 effective. Then you add market-rate rent in Sacramento, somewhere between $2,000 and $3,000 a month. PG&E and SMUD are another $200 to $300 a month on top of that.

After-housing scenario Left per year Left per month
Best case ($2k rent, $200 power)$39,300$3,275
Mid ($2.5k rent, $250 power)$33,300$2,775
Worst case ($3k rent, $300 power)$26,100$2,175

That looks like an upgrade on paper. But it ignores three things.

First, you lose Medicare. You lose the housing subsidy. You lose the low-income tax credits. Those are not easy to get back. Once you earn enough to qualify for nothing, the door closes behind you and the waitlist is years long if you ever need it again.

Second, you pick up the entire administrative burden of private insurance, income reporting, benefit reapplication, and the permanent anxiety that one bad month could wipe out whatever cushion you built.

Third, and this is the part nobody puts in a spreadsheet: you stop feeling human.

The difference in disposable income is something like $1,500 to $2,000 a month. It's real money. But it's not life-changing money. Not for the price of losing the only safety net you have and signing up for a permanent game of administrative whack-a-mole.

The hidden cost

I told the guy from New Leaf Solutions I was making $230 by working. He ran the same numbers I did and confirmed it. $230 a month. That's what the whole song and dance came down to. He asked if I'd consider working more to qualify for SSDI, and I told him I already made the decision.

He has a master's degree in institutional bureaucratic trauma. Which is a hell of a thing to specialize in if you think about it. The fact that there is a field of study for "how institutions traumatize people" tells you everything you need to know about whether this is working as intended.

And even he fell back on "well if you worked more" without hearing that the number was never the point.

The en passant solution

I still take on work. I just route it through my sister's Venmo into an ABLE account. It's legal. The system didn't anticipate someone threading a needle through two accounts and a family member to do real work for real people, so there isn't a rule against it. The SSA workers who know what's up don't ask. They know the math doesn't work. They know the letters are automated and the system can't keep up. So they look the other way and tell you to ignore the notices until the number proves something is wrong.

"I know we don't give you enough." A claims rep said that to me when I was 20. He asked what I'd do if the money ran out mid-month. I said I could do freelance web development. He said good, because he knew it wasn't enough. That was the real training. Not the certification. The moment someone inside the system told me I had to operate outside it.

The real reason

People assume this is about the money. It's not. $230 a month is not worth my dignity. The $400 gap between SSI and whatever I'd get otherwise is trivial. I could sneeze and make that much overnight.

The cost is the fragmentation. The agencies that don't talk to each other. The accounting departments that need legal to find a "path forward" for programs that have existed for years. The re-explaining of the same situation in twenty different frameworks to twenty different people who can't step outside their own box. Being the translation layer on top of your own survival.

I haven't felt human for months. That's the number that matters. And when I realized the pathway out was just a longer tunnel, I decided I'd rather work on the things I care about for the people I care about than spend my life hoping the system changes before I'm dead.

I'm alive now. I'm going to treat it that way.
